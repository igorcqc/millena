# Purpose

This skill defines how to use testimonials, reviews, numbers, and authority signals to build trust and reduce skepticism.

Social proof works because people look to others' behavior to decide what is safe or valuable, especially under uncertainty.

---

# Scope

Applies to any trust-building element: testimonials, case studies, ratings, client logos, usage numbers, media mentions, certifications.

# Types of Social Proof

## Testimonials

Direct quotes from real customers describing their experience or results.

Strongest when specific, not generic ("They responded in one day and solved my issue" beats "Great service!").

---

## Case Studies

Detailed before/after stories showing the transformation achieved.

Best for high-ticket and B2B offers where the buying decision requires more justification.

---

## Numbers and Statistics

Quantifiable proof: "500+ projects delivered", "4.9/5 average rating", "10,000 businesses trust us".

Numbers must be real and, when possible, specific rather than rounded ("1,247 clients" can feel more credible than "1,000+").

---

## Client / Partner Logos

Recognizable brands or a volume of logos signal credibility, especially for B2B and high-ticket offers.

---

## Ratings and Reviews

Star ratings and review counts from recognized platforms (Google, Trustpilot, industry-specific platforms).

---

## Media Mentions

Press features, "as seen in" sections.

---

## Certifications and Authority

Licenses, awards, professional affiliations relevant to the industry.

---

# Placement Strategy

Social proof should not be confined to a single "testimonials section."

Distribute it:

- Early (hero or right after) — a light trust signal to reduce initial skepticism
- Mid-page — detailed testimonials/case studies after benefits are explained
- Near the CTA — proof right before asking for action reduces last-moment hesitation

---

# Selecting Proof Content

Choose testimonials and case studies that:

- Match the visitor's likely situation (same industry, similar problem)
- Include specific results, not vague praise
- Represent the range of objections the page needs to overcome

Avoid using only the most extreme, unrepresentative results — they can feel unbelievable.

---

# Format Rules

- Use real names and, when permitted, photos or company names — anonymous quotes carry less weight
- Keep testimonial excerpts focused; trim to the most relevant part
- Video testimonials outperform text when available, but text must always be present as a fallback

---

# Common Mistakes

Avoid:

- Generic, vague testimonials ("Awesome company!")
- Fake or exaggerated numbers
- Overloading the page with too many testimonials, diluting impact
- Placing all social proof in one section instead of distributing it
- Using stock-photo "testimonials" with no real attribution

---

# Implementation Rules

The AI must:

1. Identify which objections social proof needs to overcome.
2. Select or request proof content matching the target audience's situation.
3. Distribute proof across the page rather than isolating it in one block.
4. Prefer specific, verifiable claims over vague praise.
5. Never fabricate testimonials, numbers, or logos.

---

# Quality Checklist

□ Proof is specific, not generic

□ Proof matches the target audience's context

□ Distributed across multiple sections, not just one

□ Real attribution (names, photos, or companies) when possible

□ No exaggerated or unverifiable claims

---

# AI Instructions

When real proof content is not provided, do not invent it.

Instead, mark placeholders clearly (e.g., "[Insert real testimonial from a client in this industry]") and flag to the user that real content is needed before publishing.
